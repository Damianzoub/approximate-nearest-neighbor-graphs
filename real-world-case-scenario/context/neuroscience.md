# Neuroscience

Neuroscience is the scientific study of the nervous system — its structure, function, development, genetics, pharmacology, and pathology. The brain, containing approximately 86 billion neurons and trillions of synaptic connections, is the most complex known biological structure. Understanding the brain is central to medicine, psychology, and the development of artificial intelligence.

## Brain Structure and Function

The human brain is divided into distinct regions with specialised functions. The cerebral cortex — the outermost layer — is responsible for higher-order functions including perception, language, reasoning, and voluntary movement. It is divided into four lobes: the frontal lobe (executive function, motor control), parietal lobe (sensory processing, spatial awareness), temporal lobe (auditory processing, memory, language), and occipital lobe (visual processing).

The limbic system, including the amygdala and hippocampus, governs emotion, memory formation, and fear responses. The basal ganglia regulate motor control and habit formation. The cerebellum coordinates movement and balance. The brainstem controls fundamental life functions — breathing, heart rate, and consciousness.

## Neurons and Synaptic Transmission

Neurons are the fundamental units of the nervous system. Each neuron has a cell body (soma), branching dendrites that receive signals from other neurons, and a long axon that transmits signals to other cells. When a neuron is sufficiently stimulated, it fires an action potential — an electrical signal that travels along the axon.

At the synapse, the junction between neurons, the action potential triggers the release of chemical neurotransmitters from the presynaptic terminal. These molecules bind to receptors on the postsynaptic neuron, either exciting or inhibiting it. Glutamate is the primary excitatory neurotransmitter; GABA is the primary inhibitory one. Dopamine, serotonin, norepinephrine, and acetylcholine play crucial roles in mood, motivation, attention, and learning.

## Neuroplasticity

The brain is not a fixed, static organ but continuously reorganises itself in response to experience — a property called neuroplasticity. Long-term potentiation (LTP) strengthens synaptic connections between neurons that fire together, the cellular basis of learning and memory. Neuroplasticity allows recovery after stroke or injury and underlies the effects of education, practice, and therapy.

During early development, the brain undergoes massive growth and pruning, with experience shaping which circuits are strengthened and which are eliminated. Adult neurogenesis — the generation of new neurons — occurs in the hippocampus and is associated with learning and mood regulation.

## Neurological and Psychiatric Disorders

Neurological disorders — Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, epilepsy, stroke, and multiple sclerosis — affect hundreds of millions worldwide. Alzheimer's is characterised by amyloid plaques and tau tangles that disrupt neuronal function and communication, leading to progressive memory loss and cognitive decline. Parkinson's results from the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, causing motor symptoms including tremor and rigidity.

Psychiatric disorders including depression, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and anxiety disorders involve dysregulation of neurotransmitter systems and circuit-level dysfunction. Antidepressants, antipsychotics, and mood stabilisers modulate neurotransmitter activity. Psychotherapy rewires maladaptive thought patterns through neuroplastic mechanisms.

## Brain-Computer Interfaces

Brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) establish direct communication pathways between the brain and external devices. Invasive BCIs, such as Neuralink's N1 chip, implant electrodes directly into cortical tissue to record individual neuron activity with high precision. Non-invasive BCIs, such as EEG-based systems, record brain signals from the scalp but with lower spatial resolution.

BCIs are being developed to restore movement to paralysed patients, enable communication for people with locked-in syndrome, and potentially treat neurological and psychiatric conditions through closed-loop stimulation. Long-term, BCIs raise profound questions about the boundary between human cognition and machine intelligence.
