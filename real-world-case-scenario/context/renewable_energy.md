# Renewable Energy

Renewable energy comes from sources that are naturally replenished on a human timescale — sunlight, wind, rain, tides, waves, and geothermal heat. Unlike fossil fuels, renewables produce little to no greenhouse gas emissions during operation, making them central to global decarbonisation efforts. Costs have fallen dramatically: solar photovoltaic (PV) power is now the cheapest source of electricity in history in most regions.

## Solar Power

Photovoltaic cells convert sunlight directly into electricity through the photovoltaic effect. Silicon-based solar panels dominate the market, but perovskite solar cells are emerging as a potentially cheaper and higher-efficiency alternative. Concentrated Solar Power (CSP) uses mirrors or lenses to focus sunlight onto a receiver that generates heat, driving a steam turbine.

The cost of utility-scale solar has fallen by over 90% since 2010, making it competitive with natural gas and coal in most markets. Solar capacity additions are now the largest of any electricity source globally. Rooftop solar allows homes and businesses to generate their own power and export surplus to the grid.

## Wind Energy

Wind turbines convert the kinetic energy of moving air into electricity. Onshore wind is among the cheapest sources of new electricity generation in most countries. Offshore wind farms, though more expensive to build, benefit from stronger and more consistent winds and can be sited away from populated areas. Turbine heights and blade lengths have grown dramatically, with some offshore turbines exceeding 250 metres in height.

Floating offshore wind platforms unlock access to deep-water sites with excellent wind resources, particularly relevant for countries like Japan, South Korea, and the UK. Wind energy generated around 6% of global electricity in 2023 and is growing rapidly.

## Energy Storage

Intermittency — the fact that solar and wind generate power only when the sun shines or wind blows — is the central challenge for a renewable-dominated grid. Lithium-ion batteries, the technology behind electric vehicles, are increasingly deployed at grid scale for short-duration storage (hours). Costs have fallen over 90% since 2010, making battery storage economically viable.

Longer-duration storage technologies being developed include pumped hydropower, iron-air batteries, flow batteries, compressed air energy storage, and green hydrogen. Green hydrogen, produced by electrolysing water using renewable electricity, can store energy for weeks or months and substitute for fossil fuels in industry, shipping, and heavy transport.

## Green Hydrogen

Hydrogen has long been used in industrial processes but is currently produced primarily from natural gas (grey hydrogen), releasing CO₂. Green hydrogen produced from renewable electricity has negligible emissions. Electrolysers — devices that split water into hydrogen and oxygen — are becoming cheaper as manufacturing scales up.

Green hydrogen is seen as a crucial decarbonisation tool for hard-to-electrify sectors: steel production, cement, shipping, aviation, and long-haul trucking. Countries including Germany, Australia, Chile, and Saudi Arabia are pursuing large-scale green hydrogen production and export strategies.

## Grid Modernisation

The energy transition requires transforming electricity grids designed around centralised fossil fuel plants into flexible systems that can integrate distributed, variable renewable generation. Smart grids use digital sensors, communication, and control systems to optimise power flows in real time.

Demand response programmes incentivise consumers to shift electricity use to times of low demand or high renewable generation. Virtual Power Plants (VPPs) aggregate distributed resources — rooftop solar, batteries, electric vehicles — and coordinate them as a single flexible resource for grid operators.
