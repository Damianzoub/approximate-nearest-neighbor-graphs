# Climate Change

Climate change refers to long-term shifts in global temperatures and weather patterns. While some natural climate variability has always occurred, since the mid-20th century human activities — primarily the burning of fossil fuels — have become the dominant driver of climate change. The scientific consensus, supported by data from thousands of researchers worldwide, confirms that Earth's average surface temperature has risen approximately 1.2°C above pre-industrial levels.

## Causes and Greenhouse Gases

The primary cause of current climate change is the emission of greenhouse gases (GHGs), particularly carbon dioxide (CO₂), methane (CH₄), and nitrous oxide (N₂O). When fossil fuels — coal, oil, and natural gas — are burned for energy, they release CO₂ that had been sequestered underground for millions of years. Deforestation compounds the problem by removing trees that absorb CO₂. Agriculture and livestock farming are significant sources of methane and nitrous oxide.

The greenhouse effect works by allowing solar radiation to pass through the atmosphere while trapping heat that radiates from Earth's surface. Without any greenhouse effect, Earth's average temperature would be around -18°C. The enhanced greenhouse effect from human emissions is disrupting this balance, driving warming at an unprecedented rate.

## Observed Impacts

The consequences of climate change are already visible across the globe. Arctic sea ice is declining at roughly 13% per decade. Glaciers on every continent are retreating, threatening freshwater supplies for hundreds of millions of people. Sea levels are rising at approximately 3.6 mm per year, with acceleration projected through the century.

Extreme weather events — heatwaves, droughts, wildfires, and intense hurricanes — are becoming more frequent and severe. Coral reef bleaching events have increased dramatically due to ocean warming and acidification. Biodiversity loss is accelerating as species struggle to adapt to rapidly shifting habitats.

## Mitigation Strategies

Limiting warming to 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels — the target set by the 2015 Paris Agreement — requires reducing net global GHG emissions to zero by approximately 2050. This demands a rapid transition away from fossil fuels toward renewable energy sources, electrification of transport and heating, energy efficiency improvements, and carbon capture technologies.

Nature-based solutions, including reforestation, wetland restoration, and improved land management, can sequester significant amounts of carbon. Carbon pricing mechanisms — carbon taxes and cap-and-trade systems — create economic incentives to reduce emissions across industries.

## Adaptation

Even with aggressive mitigation, some degree of climate change is now unavoidable. Adaptation strategies include building sea walls and restoring coastal ecosystems, developing drought-resistant crop varieties, improving early warning systems for extreme weather events, and redesigning urban infrastructure to cope with higher temperatures and increased flooding.

Climate justice is a key consideration: the nations and communities least responsible for historical emissions are often the most vulnerable to climate impacts. International finance and technology transfer to developing nations are central to a fair global response.
